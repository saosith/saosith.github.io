## What it is for

On-chip references, sensor front-ends and switched-capacitor stages all need a
general-purpose amplifier that is unconditionally stable into a small capacitive
load and cheap enough to instantiate several times. That is the brief here: a
two-stage Miller-compensated op-amp with enough loop gain to keep a unity-gain
buffer's error below one LSB of a 12-bit converter, driving 2 pF, on a 20 µA
bias.

The specification above is written before the circuit, not after it. That
ordering is the point — a design either meets a number it committed to, or it
does not.

## Topology

Two stages, because one cannot supply 100 dB at this bias in a 180 nm-class
process without cascoding away the output swing. A differential pair with a PMOS
active load provides the first stage, a common-source stage provides the second,
and a Miller capacitor with a nulling resistor makes the pair of them stable.

## Sizing

Sizing this topology is not a search; it is four decisions taken in order.
Transconductance of the input pair falls out of the bandwidth target, the Miller
capacitor out of the phase-margin target, the second-stage current out of where
the non-dominant pole has to land, and device areas out of the offset budget.

The explorer below runs the hand model those decisions are made with. Move the
tail current and watch bandwidth and power move together; move the Miller
capacitor and watch phase margin trade against slew rate.

<DesignExplorer
  figNo="Fig. 2"
  caption="Design-space explorer. Sliders drive a first-order square-law model; the plot is the model's own open-loop response, redrawn on every change."
/>

The shipped sizing sits at 20 µA tail, (W/L)₁ = 26, CC = 1.6 pF, and a second
stage running seven times the tail current. Raising the tail current buys
bandwidth and slew rate as √I while power rises linearly, and it *costs* DC
gain — output resistance falls as 1/I, faster than transconductance rises.

Phase margin barely moves with tail current, which surprises people: the second
stage is scaled to the first, so the crossover and the output pole shift
together. Phase margin is set by the Miller capacitor and the load, not by the
bias. Drag CC down and watch it collapse.

## Frequency response across corners

Five corners, one plot. The reference trace is the typical corner; the corner
that matters is the slow one, because that is where the second pole is lowest
and phase margin is thinnest.

<BodeFigure
  figNo="Fig. 3"
  caption="Open-loop gain and phase against frequency, five process corners. The marker is the unity-gain crossover of the typical corner; the vertical bar on the phase panel is its phase margin. Use the legend to isolate a corner."
/>

Gain and bandwidth move in opposite directions across the corners, which is what
should happen: the slow corner has higher output resistance and therefore more DC
gain, but less transconductance and therefore less bandwidth. Worst-case phase
margin is 64.6° at SS, comfortably inside the 60° target, and the spread in
crossover frequency is 22.1 to 27.4 MHz — about ±11 % around typical.

## Mismatch

Corners cover process shift; they say nothing about two nominally identical
devices on the same die being different. That is a separate 500-point run with
process fixed and only mismatch enabled.

<MonteCarloFigure
  figNo="Fig. 4"
  caption="Input-referred offset over 500 mismatch samples. Bars outside the ±5 mV specification window are drawn in red; the vertical rule marks the sample mean."
/>

This is the parameter that misses. Standard deviation is 2.23 mV, so a 3σ
part sits at ±6.7 mV against a ±5 mV budget, and 3.4 % of samples fall outside
the window. Offset is dominated by threshold mismatch in the input pair, which
scales as 1/√(WL) — closing the gap means roughly doubling the input-pair area,
which costs input capacitance and a little bandwidth. That trade is the next
revision, not a tuning exercise.

## What went wrong

The first version of this amplifier simulated at 71° of phase margin and rang
badly in transient. The AC response was not lying; it was answering a different
question. Phase margin had been read off the *open-loop* plot while the ringing
was a *closed-loop* step response into a load the AC testbench did not have —
the testbench drove a 2 pF capacitor, the transient testbench drove 2 pF plus
the scope-probe model, about 4 pF total.

Two lessons stuck. Load capacitance belongs in the conditions line of every
result, not in the testbench file where nobody reads it; that is why every table
on this site carries one. And the non-dominant pole moves with total load, so a
phase margin quoted without its load is not a specification. Re-running the AC
sweep at the true 4 pF gave 61°, which matched the transient overshoot.

## Limitations and next steps

- **Temperature is not swept.** Every result here is at 27 °C. Phase margin at
  the slow corner will get worse hot, and that is the corner with the least
  headroom.
- **No extracted parasitics.** These are schematic-level results. Layout
  parasitics on the compensation node will move the zero.
- **Offset misses its target** by about 1.7 mV at 3σ, as above.
- **No PSRR or CMRR data yet.** The tail cascode was chosen with CMRR in mind but
  the number has not been taken.

## Reproducing this

The netlists and the testbench are in the repository linked in the sidebar. The
plots on this page are generated from the raw simulator output rather than
redrawn by hand:

```bash
# AC sweep, one rawfile per corner
ngspice -b -r sim/ac_TT.raw sim/opamp_ac.spice

# convert the rawfiles into the JSON these charts read
node scripts/raw-to-json.mjs \
  --out public/data/opamp-ac.json \
  --node "v(out)" \
  --source simulated \
  --conditions "VDD = 1.8 V, T = 27 °C, CL = 2 pF" \
  TT=sim/ac_TT.raw FF=sim/ac_FF.raw SS=sim/ac_SS.raw \
  FS=sim/ac_FS.raw SF=sim/ac_SF.raw
```

The converter derives DC gain, the unity-gain crossover and phase margin from
the curve itself, so the summary table under each plot cannot drift away from
the trace above it.
